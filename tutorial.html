Small-Bias Sets in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\)

How to choose a few clock hands, each turning at its own speed, that are spread around the dial at every moment, with no randomness, and prove it.

The whole proof

The whole proof in plain words

The goal

Each number \(k\) in our list is a clock hand that turns \(\frac{k}{n}\) of a full turn per tick. All hands start pointing right. A good list is one where, whenever you look at the clock (any time 1 to \(n-1\)), the hands are spread around the dial, so their average lands near the centre.

The recipe

Mix two small ingredients: a few dozen primes, and a short list of small whole numbers, the multipliers, found by a greedy search. The final list holds every multiplier divided by every prime, with the division done modulo \(n\).

Why it works

Split the hands into groups, one per prime. Each group has a base hand, and every hand in the group turns a whole number of times as fast as it, so at any moment it points at that multiple of the base hand's angle. The multipliers are chosen so that whenever the base hand is not pointing almost straight right, the group is spread out. A group's base hand can only point almost straight right if its prime divides the time, and a number can't have many big prime factors. So at every moment almost every group is spread out, and the few exceptions are outvoted.

The result

Error at most \(\varepsilon\) with about \(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^3}\) numbers, chosen with no randomness. A computer (the Lean proof assistant) has checked every step.

Credits

The prime layer and the count of bad groups come from Ajtai, Iwaniec, Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi (1990). The greedy search is Raghavan's method of pessimistic estimators (1988). Here one multiplier list, found once by search, serves every prime. Full list: References.

Overview

The recipe at a glance

\[ S = \{\, c \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n \;:\; q \in P,\ c \in C \,\} \]
  • \(P\): \(|P|\) primes slightly larger than \(R \approx \frac{4 \ln n}{\varepsilon}\). Each prime \(q\) gives one group of hands, whose base hand is the hand \(q^{-1}\).
  • \(C\): \(s\) small whole numbers, the multipliers, chosen one at a time by a greedy algorithm. So the list has \(m = |P| \cdot s\) hands.

Size. Bigger primes mean fewer groups, \(|P| \approx \frac{3 \ln n}{\varepsilon \ln R}\), but more multipliers per group, \(s \approx (\text{a few}) \cdot \frac{\ln R}{\varepsilon^2}\). The \(\ln R\) cancels, so \(m = O\big(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^3}\big)\). The primes shrink \(n-1\) moments to one small problem about the multipliers, which a search solves once and every group reuses.

Credit. The prime layer, with groups \(c \cdot q^{-1}\) for many primes \(q\), is the method of Ajtai, Iwaniec, Komlós, Pintz & Szemerédi (1990). They fill each group recursively; here one shared list of multipliers is found by greedy search, using Raghavan's pessimistic estimators (1988). References

Part 1

The problem from scratch

Every number in the list is a clock hand turning at its own speed. All hands start pointing right. When we look at the clock at some time \(\ell\), a good list has its hands spread around the dial, so their average sits near the centre.

Simple lists, like 0 to 9 or the powers of two, always have some moment when their hands bunch up. Even lists built from good binary linear codes, which pass every XOR test, can have such a moment. Random lists are spread out at every moment with high probability, but we want a recipe that is guaranteed to work and is fast to run.

Part 1 · The problem

1.1 Hands on a clock

Write \(e(x)\) for the tip of a hand of length 1 that starts pointing right and has turned \(x\) full turns counter-clockwise.

  • \(e(0)\) points right, \(e(\frac14)\) up, \(e(\frac12)\) left, \(e(\frac34)\) down.
  • Whole turns don't matter: \(e(x + 1) = e(x)\).

A number \(k\) in the list is a hand that turns \(\frac{k}{n}\) of a turn per tick. At time \(\ell\) it has turned \(\frac{\ell k}{n}\) turns, so it points at \(e\big(\frac{\ell k}{n}\big)\). That is the expression \(e^{2\pi i k \ell / n}\) from the original question.

Part 1 · The problem

1.2 Looking at time \(\ell\): the score

  • At time \(\ell\), hand \(k\) points at \(e\big(\frac{\ell k}{n}\big)\).
  • The average arrow \(\hat S(\ell)\) is the centre of mass of all the hand tips at time \(\ell\). The score is its length \(|\hat S(\ell)|\): 0 means perfectly spread, 1 means all hands together.
  • At time 0 every hand points right, so the score is 1. That moment doesn't count.
  • At time \(n\) every hand has made a whole number of turns, so the clock repeats every \(n\) ticks. Only the times \(1, \dots, n-1\) matter.
  • The bias of the list is its worst score over those times. A list with bias \(\le \varepsilon\) is called \(\varepsilon\)-biased, or a small-bias set.
\[ \hat S(\ell) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} e\Big(\frac{\ell\, k_j}{n}\Big), \qquad \text{bias} = \max_{1 \le \ell \le n-1} |\hat S(\ell)| \]

Credit. Small-bias sets were introduced by Naor & Naor (1990) for bit strings, where XOR tests play the role of times. Azar, Motwani & Naor (1998) studied them in \(\mathbb{Z}_n\) and other abelian groups. Number theorists call them thin sets with small Fourier coefficients (Ajtai et al. 1990). In quantum computing they are the coefficients of quantum fingerprinting (Ambainis & Nahimovs 2008). References

Part 1 · The problem

1.3 Two natural attempts that fail

  • 0, 1, …, 9. These hands are very slow. At time 1 they have turned at most \(\frac{9}{1024}\) of a turn, so they are still bunched at the start: score 0.9998.
  • Powers of two. At time \(512 = \frac{n}{2}\), hand \(2^j\) has turned \(\frac{2^j}{2}\) turns. For \(j \ge 1\) that is a whole number of turns, so nine hands point right; hand 1 has turned half a turn and points left. Score \(\frac{9-1}{10} = 0.8\).

Every structured list has some moment when its hands bunch up. Part 9 shows this is unavoidable when the list is too short.

Part 1 · The problem

1.4 Third attempt: binary linear codes

Take \(n = 2^r\) and write the \(m\) numbers in binary, as the columns of an \(r \times m\) table of bits. Row \(b\) holds bit \(b\) of every number.

  • An XOR test picks some bit positions and asks, for each number, whether an odd number of those bits are 1. The \(m\) answers are the XOR of the chosen rows: a codeword of the binary linear code spanned by the rows.
  • The test is balanced when that codeword is about half ones. Good codes make every nonzero codeword nearly balanced. Known constructions use about \(\frac{r^2}{\varepsilon^2}\) columns (AGHP; Reed–Solomon with Hadamard), about \(\frac{r}{\varepsilon^3}\) (Reed–Solomon with a small inner code found by search), and about \(\frac{r}{\varepsilon^{2+o(1)}}\) (Ta-Shma).

How it looks on the clock. At time \(\ell\), each bit \(b\) that is 1 turns the hand by its own fixed angle, \(\frac{\ell \cdot 2^b}{n}\) turns, and the hand's angle is the sum. An XOR test is the special case where every bit turns the hand by 0 or \(\frac12\). On our clock that happens only at time \(\frac{n}{2}\). At every other time some bits turn the hand by \(\frac14, \frac18, \frac{3}{16}, \dots\), and balance against "flip or don't flip" doesn't control those.

Credit. Small-bias sets as balanced codes: Naor & Naor (1990). Constructions: Alon, Goldreich, Håstad & Peralta (1992); Reed–Solomon codes (Reed & Solomon 1960) concatenated with an inner code (Forney 1966); Ta-Shma (2017). References

Part 1 · The problem

1.5 Passing every XOR test is not enough

A concrete list for \(n = 2^{12} = 4096\): keep the 512 numbers whose hand at time 405 points within \(\frac{1}{16}\) of a turn of right. These are \(405^{-1} \cdot \{-256, \dots, 255\} \bmod n\).

  • Every XOR test is balanced to within 0.352: every codeword of its code has between 32% and 68% ones.
  • At time 405 every hand is within \(\frac{1}{16}\) of a turn of right, so the score is 0.974.

Larger \(n\) makes the gap worse. The same recipe, at the best time found:

XOR balance keeps improving while one moment stays fully bunched. So XOR balance alone guarantees nothing on the clock, and the construction works with the clock directly.

Part 1 · The problem

1.6 Random lists work, so why not use one?

A list of about \(C \cdot \frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}\) random numbers has bias \(\le \varepsilon\) with high probability. Two standard facts give this:

  • Chernoff bound: at one fixed time, the average of \(m\) random hands is longer than \(\varepsilon\) with probability at most about \(e^{-c\, m\, \varepsilon^2}\), for a constant \(c\).
  • Union bound: there are fewer than \(n\) times, so it is enough that \(n \cdot e^{-c\, m\, \varepsilon^2}\) is small. That holds once \(m\) is a constant times \(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}\).

We want a deterministic recipe instead: a fixed procedure whose output is guaranteed to work, not just likely to. Checking a random list takes about \(n\) steps, which is impossible for \(n = 2^{64}\). The recipe must also be fast: polynomial in \(\log n\), never in \(n\).

Python version of the first figures 
import cmath, math

def e(x):
    return cmath.exp(2j * math.pi * x)

def score(S, n, l):
    """Length of the average hand at time l."""
    return abs(sum(e(l * k / n) for k in S)) / len(S)

def bias(S, n):
    """The worst time. Tries all n - 1 times, so only for small n."""
    return max(score(S, n, l) for l in range(1, n))

print(bias(range(10), 1024))                   # ≈ 0.9998
print(bias([2**j for j in range(10)], 1024))   # 0.8
print(bias([0, 1, 2, 3], 4))                   # ≈ 1e-16, i.e. 0 up to rounding

Credit. Random lists of about \(\frac{\log |G|}{\varepsilon^2}\) elements work in every finite group \(G\): Alon & Roichman (1994). The tail bound is Chernoff's (1952), in the form of Hoeffding (1963). References

Part 2

Toolbox

Five tools we need later:

  • dividing by a prime modulo \(n\), and why the result stays small when the division is exact;
  • reading numbers as signed, like two's complement;
  • a group of hands that each turn a whole number of times as fast as one base hand, so they always point at that multiple of its angle;
  • why the multiples \(0, 1, \dots, N-1\) are spread out unless the base hand points almost straight right;
  • a trick for keeping many running totals small at once by watching one sum; the greedy search of Part 5 relies on it.
Part 2 · Toolbox

2.1 Modular inverses

The inverse of \(q\) mod \(n\) is the number \(u\) with q*u % n == 1. It exists when \(q\) and \(n\) share no factor; for a prime \(q\), when \(q\) does not divide \(n\). In Python: pow(q, -1, n). For \(n = 1024\), \(3^{-1} = 683\), since \(3 \cdot 683 = 2 \cdot 1024 + 1\).

The one property we need: if \(q\) divides \(\ell\), then \(\ell \cdot q^{-1} \equiv \frac{\ell}{q} \pmod n\). Multiply both sides of \(q \cdot \frac{\ell}{q} = \ell\) by \(q^{-1}\) to see it.

So when \(q\) divides \(\ell\), multiplying by \(q^{-1}\) is ordinary division, and the result stays small. Otherwise it looks scrambled.

Part 2 · Toolbox

2.2 Signed numbers and distance to a whole turn

  • The signed representative \(\ell^*\). Every residue mod \(n\) has a representative in \(\big(-\frac{n}{2}, \frac{n}{2}\big]\). For \(n = 2^r\) this is the two's-complement reading of an \(r\)-bit word: 931 mod 1024 reads as −93.
  • \(\|x\|\) is the distance from \(x\) to the nearest whole number, between 0 and \(\frac12\). For the hand \(e(x)\), it says how far the hand is from pointing right, measured in turns.
  • They fit together: \(\big\|\frac{\ell}{n}\big\| = \frac{|\ell^*|}{n}\). A hand that has turned \(\frac{d}{n}\) turns points almost straight right exactly when the signed value of \(d\) is small.
Part 2 · Toolbox

2.3 A base hand and its multiples

Take one base hand and a list \(C\) of whole numbers, the multipliers. For each \(c\) in \(C\) there is a hand that turns exactly \(c\) times as fast as the base hand. All start pointing right, so at every moment: if the base hand has turned \(a\) turns, hand \(c\) has turned \(c \cdot a\) turns.

So the group's average at time \(\ell\) depends only on where the base hand points at that moment. If it points at \(a\), the average is

\[ \frac{1}{s} \sum_{c \in C} e(c \cdot a) \]

From Part 3 on, the group of prime \(q\) is the list \(S_q\), and this is its average \(\hat S_q(\ell)\) at time \(\ell\).

  • Base hand almost straight right (\(\|a\|\) tiny): hand \(c\) has turned only about \(c \cdot a\), so the group is bunched and its average has length \(\approx 1\).
  • Base hand anywhere else: the hands can spread out, and well-chosen multipliers make them cancel.
  • Base hand pointing left (\(a = \frac12\)): even multiples point right, odd ones left.

Bunched means the hands have not fanned out yet: they all sit on one side of the dial, and their average is long. That happens when the base hand points very close to right. For these 12 multipliers it is the red strip at the left of the chart, and the hands on the dial turn red while you are inside it.

The excused zone is a different strip. Pick the greedy or random multipliers and the red strip becomes the proof's zone: the base hand within \(\delta = \frac{1}{2P_{\max}}\) of a turn from right (here \(\frac{1}{26}\)). Inside it the hands may already have made several turns. The proof simply makes no promise there, and Part 4 shows why it can afford that.

Part 2 · Toolbox

2.4 The multiples \(0, 1, \dots, N-1\) spread out

For the multipliers \(C = \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\), whenever the base hand points at \(a\), the group's average satisfies

\[ \Big|\, \frac{1}{N} \sum_{c=0}^{N-1} e(c \cdot a) \,\Big| \;\le\; \frac{1}{2N\,\|a\|} \]

Intuition. The hands sit at \(0, a, 2a, \dots, (N-1)a\) turns, stepping evenly around the dial and going around about \(N a\) times. Complete laps cancel; only the last, incomplete lap is left, at most about a \(\frac{1}{Na}\) fraction of the hands.

Bunched here is the red strip \(a < \frac{1}{2N}\). There the bound \(\frac{1}{2N\|a\|}\) is above 1 and promises nothing: even the fastest hand has turned less than half a turn, so all hands are still on one side of the dial. Past the strip the bound drops quickly, and the group is spread out.

The catch: that is \(N\) hands, far too many. The proof uses \(\{0, \dots, N-1\}\) only as the pool of candidate multipliers the greedy algorithm picks from (Part 5).

Where the formula comes from

\(\sum_{c<N} z^c\) with \(z = e(a)\) is a geometric series, equal to \(\frac{z^N - 1}{z - 1}\). The numerator has length at most 2; the denominator has length \(2 \sin(\pi \|a\|) \ge 4\|a\|\). Divide by \(N\).

Part 2 · Toolbox

2.5 The exponential trick: keeping many totals small at once

Where we need it. In Part 5 a greedy algorithm picks the multipliers one at a time. While it works, it has to watch thousands of running totals at once, one per possible way the group could go wrong. Each total says how far the hands chosen so far lean in one direction, for one position of the base hand. Call them \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T\), where \(T\) is how many there are. All of them must stay small, because a large total means the group leans in that direction.

Why not just watch the largest one? The largest total ignores all the others, and there is no clean way to predict how it changes when a hand is added. So instead watch one number that sees all of them:

\[ \Phi = e^{t x_1} + e^{t x_2} + \dots + e^{t x_T} \qquad (\text{sharpness } t > 0) \]
  • Small \(\Phi\) means every total is small. Each term is at most the sum, so \(x_i \le \frac{\ln \Phi}{t}\) for every \(i\).
  • It is a soft maximum. \(\frac{\ln \Phi}{t}\) lies between the largest \(x_i\) and the largest plus \(\frac{\ln T}{t}\). A larger \(t\) follows the maximum more closely.
  • It is easy to update. Adding a hand changes each \(x_i\) by that hand's lean \(X\), which multiplies its term by \(e^{tX}\). Products like this are easy to average over candidates, which Part 5 uses to prove \(\Phi\) grows slowly.

The same idea appears as log-sum-exp, the multiplicative weights algorithm, AdaBoost's exponential loss, and the proof of the Chernoff bound.

Credit. Steering a deterministic choice with \(\Phi\) is Raghavan's method of pessimistic estimators (1988). The other uses: multiplicative weights, see Arora, Hazan & Kale (2012); AdaBoost, Freund & Schapire (1997); the Chernoff bound, Chernoff (1952). References

Part 3

Lemma 1: the hands split into groups

Our list is every multiplier \(c\) divided by every prime \(q\). Group the hands by prime. The group of \(q\) has a base hand, the hand \(q^{-1}\), and its hand \(c\) turns exactly \(c\) times as fast as that base hand.

So at time \(\ell\), each group is the picture from Part 2: its hands point at multiples of its base hand's angle. The score at time \(\ell\) is the average over the groups of how spread out each one is.

Part 3 · Lemma 1

Lemma 1: statement and why

Group \(q\) is \(S_q = \{\, c \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n : c \in C \,\}\), and \(\hat S_q(\ell)\) is its average arrow at time \(\ell\). Its base hand is the hand \(q^{-1}\), the one with multiplier 1. It is only part of the list if 1 is a multiplier; otherwise it is an imaginary hand we measure against. At time \(\ell\) it points at

\[ \frac{\ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n} \ \text{turns.} \]

Lemma 1. At every time \(\ell\):

\[ \hat S(\ell) = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{q \in P} \hat S_q(\ell), \qquad \hat S_q(\ell) = \frac{1}{s} \sum_{c \in C} e\Big(\frac{c \cdot \ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\Big). \]
  1. All groups have the same size \(s\), so the average of all hands is the average of the group averages.
  2. Hand \(c\) of group \(q\) is the number \(c \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n\). At time \(\ell\) it has turned \(\frac{\ell \cdot (c \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n)}{n}\) turns. Reducing mod \(n\) only removes whole turns, so it points at \(e\big(\frac{c \cdot \ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\big)\): \(c\) times the base hand's angle.
  3. So the group's average \(\hat S_q(\ell)\) is \(\frac{1}{s} \sum_{c} e\big(\frac{c \cdot \ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\big)\): the picture from 2.3, with the base hand at \(a = \frac{\ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\).

Consequence. An average of arrows is never longer than the average of their lengths (the triangle inequality), so \(|\hat S(\ell)| \le \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{q} |\hat S_q(\ell)|\).

Python check 
n, l = 1024, 15
P, C = [3, 5, 7, 11, 13], [0, 1, 3, 7, 12]
S = [c * pow(q, -1, n) % n for q in P for c in C]
lhs = sum(e(l * k / n) for k in S) / len(S)
rhs = sum(sum(e(c * l * pow(q, -1, n) / n) for c in C) / len(C) for q in P) / len(P)
print(abs(lhs - rhs) < 1e-12)   # True

Credit. Groups \(c \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n\) for many primes \(q\) are the construction of Ajtai, Iwaniec, Komlós, Pintz & Szemerédi (1990); Bourgain, Dilworth, Ford, Konyagin & Kutzarova (2011, Lemma 12) give a clear modern account. There, each prime gets its own multiplier list, built by applying the construction again, and \(n\) is prime. References

Part 4

Lemma 2: only a few groups are bad

A group causes trouble only when, at time \(\ell\), its base hand points almost straight right, inside the excused zone where the proof makes no promise about its hands. Call such a group bad. That can only happen when the group's prime divides \(\ell\); otherwise dividing by the prime scrambles \(\ell\).

A number below \(n\) cannot have many big prime factors: a 64-bit number has at most six above 904. So with enough primes, the bad groups are a small minority at every moment, and they cost at most a third of the error budget.

Part 4 · Lemma 2

4.1 A bad group's prime divides \(\ell\)

Let \(P_{\max}\) be the largest prime. Call \(q\) bad at time \(\ell\) if its base hand is within \(\delta = \frac{1}{2P_{\max}}\) of a turn from right: \(\big\|\frac{\ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\big\| < \delta\). The proof is the overflow rule of two's complement:

  1. Bad means \(\ell \cdot q^{-1} \bmod n\), read as a signed number \(d\), is small: \(|d| < \frac{n}{2P_{\max}}\). (Then the base hand is \(\frac{|d|}{n}\) of a turn from right.)
  2. Multiply by \(q\): \(\ell \equiv d \cdot q \pmod n\), and \(|d \cdot q| < \frac{n}{2}\) because \(q \le P_{\max}\).
  3. \(\ell^*\) and \(d \cdot q\) are two signed numbers in \(\big(-\frac{n}{2}, \frac{n}{2}\big]\) that agree mod \(n\). The window is too narrow for them to differ by a multiple of \(n\), so they are equal: \(d \cdot q\) does not overflow.
  4. So \(\ell^* = d \cdot q\), and \(q\) divides \(\ell^*\).

The converse can fail: 7 divides 1001, but \(\frac{1001}{7} = 143\), so at time 1001 the base hand of 7 is at \(\frac{143}{1024} \approx 0.14\) turns, far from right.

Credit. That only primes dividing \(\ell\) can be bad is the key step of Ajtai et al. (1990). For prime \(n\) they show it with a reciprocity identity (see Bourgain et al. 2011, Lemma 12), and Razborov, Szemerédi & Wigderson (1993) handle composite \(n\) separately. The two's-complement argument above works for every \(n\). References

Part 4 · Lemma 2

4.2 A number has only a few large prime factors

Claim. At most \(K = \big\lfloor \log_{R+1} \frac{n}{2} \big\rfloor\) primes of \(P\) are bad at any time \(\ell\).

  1. \(\ell^*\) is not 0, because \(\ell\) is not a multiple of \(n\).
  2. Every bad prime divides \(\ell^*\). They are distinct primes, so their product divides \(\ell^*\), and the product is at most \(|\ell^*| \le \frac{n}{2}\).
  3. Every prime in \(P\) is at least \(R + 1\), so the product is at least \((R+1)^{\#\text{bad}}\), which gives \(\#\text{bad} \le \log_{R+1} \frac{n}{2}\).

Example. For \(n = 2^{64}\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.2\), \(R = 904\). A nonzero number of size at most \(2^{63}\) has at most 6 prime factors above 904, because \(905^7 > 2^{63}\). So of the \(|P| = 91\) groups, at most 6 are bad at any moment.

Why start above \(R\). With small primes 2, 3, 5, 7, …, one \(\ell\) could be divisible by many of them, about \(\frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n}\). Large primes stop one moment from making more than a few groups bad.

Credit. Counting bad primes through their product is also from Ajtai et al. (1990). References

Part 4 · Lemma 2

4.3 The cost of bad groups

A bad group scores at most 1, since an average of hands is never longer than 1.

With \(|P| = \big\lfloor \frac{3K}{\varepsilon} \big\rfloor + 1 > \frac{3K}{\varepsilon}\) groups, the bad ones contribute at most \(\frac{K}{|P|} < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}\).

The miniature on the right uses only 5 tiny primes, so a single moment can make 2 of the 5 groups bad and lose 40%. Time 15 is the worst moment of this miniature list. The real construction uses many more, larger primes, so bad groups are a small fraction.

Part 5

Lemma 3: choosing the multipliers

We need a short list of multipliers such that, wherever a group's base hand points (except almost straight right), the hands at those multiples of its angle are spread out. One list serves every group at every moment, because only the base hand's angle matters. Random multipliers would do it with high probability.

To get a guarantee without luck, we use a greedy rule. Keep a danger score for everything that could go wrong, and at each step add the multiplier that keeps the total danger lowest. An average choice keeps the danger growing slowly, so the best choice does too, and at the end every danger is under control.

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.1 The goal, and why random multipliers would work

Goal. Find a short list \(C\) of multipliers from \(\{0, \dots, N-1\}\) such that, whenever a group's base hand points at an angle \(a\) with \(\delta \le \|a\| \le \frac12\), the group's average \(\frac{1}{s} \sum_{c} e(c \cdot a)\) has length \(\le \frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\). By Lemma 1 that means \(|\hat S_q(\ell)| \le \frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\) for every group that is not bad at time \(\ell\). Two obstacles:

  1. There are infinitely many angles, and an algorithm can only check finitely many things.
  2. We want \(s\) multipliers with \(s\) much smaller than \(N\). All of \(\{0, \dots, N-1\}\) would work, but it is far too long.

Random would work. Pick \(s\) multipliers at random from the pool.

  • For a fixed base angle, each hand is a random arrow whose average is almost the centre (2.4).
  • The average of \(s\) such arrows behaves like a random walk divided by \(s\), so its length is about \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\).
  • By the Chernoff bound, the chance that it reaches further than \(\varepsilon\) in one fixed direction is at most about \(e^{-s \varepsilon^2 / 2}\).
  • With \(T\) checks (next slide), the union bound says \(s \approx \frac{2 \ln T}{\varepsilon^2}\) passes all of them at once.

The greedy algorithm is this argument with the randomness taken out.

Credit. Random multipliers work by the argument of Alon & Roichman (1994). Wigderson & Xiao (2008) removed the randomness from that argument with the same kind of greedy search, in time polynomial in the size of the group. Khadieva & Ziatdinov (2022) ran that search directly on \(\mathbb{Z}_p\) to build quantum fingerprinting automata, with one check for every time \(1, \dots, p-1\), which takes time polynomial in \(p\). Here the search only runs on the small multiplier problem, so the time is polynomial in \(\log n\). References

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.2 Making the checks finite

(a) A grid of base angles. Only check \(a = \frac{j}{M}\) with \(\frac{\delta}{2} \le a \le \frac12\). Turning the base hand by \(\frac{1}{M}\) of a turn moves hand \(c\) by \(\frac{c}{M}\) of a turn, with \(c < N\), so the average moves by at most \(\frac{2\pi N}{M}\). With \(M = \big\lceil \frac{1000 N}{\varepsilon} \big\rceil\) that is under \(0.0064\, \varepsilon\).

(b) Compass directions. The exponential trick needs numbers that add up when we add a hand, and lengths don't. So measure how far the average arrow reaches in each of \(D = 16\) directions.

  • \(X_{a,u}(c) = \operatorname{Re}\big(\omega_u \cdot e(c \cdot a)\big) \in [-1, 1]\) is the reach of hand \(c\) in direction \(u\), with \(\omega_u = e\big(-\frac{u}{D}\big)\). Reaches add up.
  • An arrow of length \(L\) is within 11.25° of some direction, so its reach there is \(\ge 0.98\, L\).

A check is one (grid angle, direction) pair. There are \(T \le 16\,(M + 1)\) checks.

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.3 One check up close

A check asks one question: if a group's base hand pointed at angle \(a\), would its hands lean too far toward direction \(u\)? Each chosen multiplier \(c\) adds a hand at \(c \cdot a\), and its reach \(X_{a,u}(c)\) is how far that hand sticks out along the spoke, between −1 and 1. The check keeps a running total of these reaches, and its danger is \(e^{t \cdot \text{total}}\). Only a large positive total is dangerous, so opposite directions, like 0° and 180°, are separate checks. \(\Phi\) is the sum of all the dangers, and each greedy step tries every candidate and keeps the one that makes \(\Phi\) smallest.

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.4 The algorithm

For each check, keep a running total: how far the hands chosen so far reach in that check's direction, when the base hand is at that check's angle. The danger of a check is \(e^{t \cdot \text{total}}\). Each step adds the multiplier that keeps the total danger smallest.

totals = [0] * T                          # one running total per check
C = []
repeat s times:
    for every candidate multiplier c in 0 .. N-1:
        Φ(c) = Σ over checks i of exp(t · (totals[i] + X_i(c)))
    c_best = the smallest c with the smallest Φ(c)    # ties → smallest c: deterministic
    C.append(c_best)
    totals[i] += X_i(c_best) for every check i

Nothing here is random. Ties go to the smallest \(c\), so the list is fully determined by the parameters.

5.5 Watch it run

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.6 Why the danger grows slowly

At the start every total is 0, so \(\Phi = T\). Now imagine adding a random multiplier instead of the best one. Each check's danger is multiplied by \(e^{tX}\), where \(X\) is that check's new reach.

  1. \(X\) has average almost 0. Over all candidates \(c < N\), the average reach is at most \(\frac{1}{2Na} \le \frac{1}{N\delta}\), by 2.4, because every grid angle is at least \(\frac{\delta}{2}\) from right.
  2. Hoeffding's lemma. If \(X\) lies in \([-1, 1]\) with average \(\mu\), the average of \(e^{tX}\) is at most \(\cosh t + |\mu| \sinh t \le e^{t^2/2 \,+\, |\mu| \sinh t}\). Intuitively \(e^{tX} \approx 1 + tX + \frac{t^2 X^2}{2}\), and \(X^2 \le 1\).

So a random multiplier multiplies \(\Phi\), on average, by at most

\[ F = \exp\Big( \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{\sinh t}{N \delta} \Big). \]

The key step. The best candidate is at least as good as the average one. So greedy multiplies \(\Phi\) by at most \(F\) per step, and after \(s\) steps \(\Phi \le T \cdot F^s\). This is the method of conditional expectations. The lower chart on the previous slide shows the actual \(\ln \Phi\) staying under this line.

Credit. Taking a choice at least as good as the average is the method of conditional expectations (Erdős & Selfridge 1973). With an exponential \(\Phi\) it is Raghavan's method of pessimistic estimators (1988). Hoeffding's lemma is from Hoeffding (1963). References

Part 5 · Lemma 3

5.7 Reading off the result

Every check's danger is at most \(\Phi\), so \(e^{t \cdot \text{total}} \le T \cdot F^s\). Take logarithms and divide by \(t \cdot s\); \(\frac{\text{total}}{s}\) is the reach of the average arrow:

\[ \text{reach} \;\le\; B = \frac{\ln T}{t\, s} + \frac{t}{2} + \frac{\sinh t}{t\, N\, \delta} \]
  • \(\frac{\ln T}{t s}\) pays for the many checks. Choosing \(s = \big\lceil \frac{2 \ln T}{t^2} \big\rceil\) makes it at most \(\frac{t}{2}\). This is the \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\) from random sampling.
  • \(\frac{t}{2}\) is the price of the exponential. With \(t = \frac{5\varepsilon}{8}\), the first two terms total at most \(0.625\, \varepsilon\).
  • \(\frac{\sinh t}{t N \delta}\) is the pool's leftover average. With \(N = \big\lceil \frac{300\, P_{\max}}{\varepsilon} \big\rceil\) it is at most \(\frac{\varepsilon}{100}\).

Converting reach to length (÷ 0.98) and adding the grid error (\(+\,0.0064\, \varepsilon\)): at every time \(\ell\), every group whose base hand points at an angle \(a\) with \(\delta \le \|a\| \le \frac12\) has

\[ |\hat S_q(\ell)| = \Big|\, \frac{1}{s} \sum_{c} e(c \cdot a) \,\Big| \;\le\; \frac{0.625\,\varepsilon + 0.01\,\varepsilon}{0.98} + 0.0064\,\varepsilon \;<\; 0.655\,\varepsilon \;<\; \frac{2\varepsilon}{3} \]
Part 6

Lemma 4: there are enough primes

We need enough primes to pick from, and they must not divide \(n\), or they have no inverse.

Primes are plentiful: between \(R\) and \(R^2\) there are many more than we need, even after removing the ones up to \(R\) and the few that divide \(n\). A classical estimate of how many primes there are, Chebyshev's bound, makes this precise.

Part 6 · Lemma 4

Counting primes

We need \(|P|\) primes in \((R, R^2]\) that do not divide \(n\).

  • Chebyshev's bound, which is in Mathlib: there are at least \(\frac{x \ln 2}{\ln x}\) primes up to \(x\). For \(x = R^2\) and \(R \ge 16\), that is at least \(2R\).
  • At most \(R + 1\) of them are \(\le R\).
  • At most \(\log_{R+1} n\) primes above \(R\) divide \(n\), by the same product argument as in 4.2.

That leaves at least \(R - 1 - \log_{R+1} n\) usable primes, and \(R = \big\lceil \frac{4 \ln n}{\varepsilon} \big\rceil + 16\) makes this at least \(|P|\).

In practice primes are far denser than this worst case: for \(n = 2^{64}\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.2\), all 91 primes lie between 905 and \(P_{\max} = 1553\).

Credit. Bounds of this kind go back to Chebyshev (1852). References

Part 7

Putting it together

At any moment, the few bad groups cost at most a third of the allowed error, and the spread-out groups at most two thirds.

The list size comes out as about \(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^3}\). Bigger primes mean fewer groups but more multipliers per group, and the two effects cancel. The proof's constants are generous safety margins, so the guaranteed size is much larger than what is needed in practice.

Part 7 · Together

The theorem, and the proof in four lines

For \(n \ge 2\) and \(0 < \varepsilon \le 1\), the construction is fully determined, runs in time polynomial in \(\log n\) and \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\), and outputs a list \(S\) with

\[ \text{bias}(S) \le \varepsilon \qquad \text{and} \qquad |S| = |P| \cdot s \;\le\; \frac{235\,(\ln n + 2)}{\varepsilon^3} \]

Fix any time \(\ell \ne 0\).

  1. Lemma 1: \(|\hat S(\ell)| \le\) the average over the \(|P|\) groups of \(|\hat S_q(\ell)|\).
  2. Lemma 2: at most \(K\) groups are bad, each contributing at most 1, so together at most \(\frac{K}{|P|} < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}\).
  3. Lemma 3: every other group has its base hand at \(\delta \le \big\|\frac{\ell \cdot q^{-1}}{n}\big\| \le \frac12\), so \(|\hat S_q(\ell)| \le 0.655\, \varepsilon < \frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\).
  4. Total: less than \(\frac{\varepsilon}{3} + \frac{2\varepsilon}{3} = \varepsilon\). ∎

Lemma 4 makes sure the \(|P|\) primes exist.

Part 8

The practical code and its certificate

The Python code uses the same primes but searches for multipliers far less thoroughly, so it runs in seconds.

The small search loses the proof's guarantee, so the code then proves a bound for the specific list it built, by checking every base-hand angle precisely. That bound is called the certificate, and it is just as rigorous.

Part 8 · The code

Build, then certify

  • formal_parameters(n, ε) computes the theorem's exact parameters and size. It runs no search.
  • build(n, ε) uses the same primes but a smaller search: \(N = 16\, P_{\max}\) candidate multipliers, about \(4N\) grid angles, 8 directions, and an FFT to score all candidates at once.

The certificate is a rigorous upper bound on the bias of the list that was built:

  1. Compute \(W(x)\), the longest group average \(\frac{1}{s} \sum_{c} e(c \cdot a)\) over all base angles \(a\) at least \(x\) from right, for every \(x\), by an exact FFT on a fine grid plus a bound on movement between grid points.
  2. Bound how many groups can have their base hand within \(x\) of right at any time, for every \(x\): a stretched Lemma 2, where \(\ell^* - j \cdot n\) has few large prime factors for each of the few wrap-arounds \(j\).
  3. Combine all distances: a group whose base hand is fairly close to right is charged \(W\) at its own distance, instead of the full 1.
import smallbias as sb
n = 2**20
res = sb.build(n, 0.3)                    # about 1 second
S = sb.build_set(n, res['P'], res['C'])
print(len(S), res['k'], res['s'])         # 1386 = 21 primes × 66 multipliers
print(res['certificate'])                 # 0.296: proved bound for this S
print(sb.measured_bias(S, n))             # 0.137: the exact bias, by FFT over all n times

Part 9

What is impossible

Some things are impossible for every list. With only about \(\log n\) hands, there is always some moment when they bunch up, so the error stays large.

To get a small error you need a multiple of \(\log n\) hands, and the multiple must grow as the error you want shrinks.

Part 9 · Limits

Three lower bounds

lower-bounds.md proves these for every list of \(m\) hands:

  • Energy (counting pairs). The squared scores over all \(n\) times add up to at least \(\frac{n}{m}\). Time 0 takes exactly 1, and the other \(n - 1\) times share the rest, so one of them gets at least about \(\frac{1}{m}\). The bias is at least about \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\), and error \(\varepsilon\) needs at least about \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\) hands.
  • Pigeonhole. With fewer than \(\frac{\log_2 n}{3}\) hands, there is a moment when all of them point within \(\frac18\) of a turn of right, so the bias is above 0.707. This is the general form of the powers-of-two failure.
  • Moments. With exactly \(\log_2 n\) hands, the bias is always at least about 0.3.

So any good list has \(C(\varepsilon) \cdot \log n\) hands, with \(C(\varepsilon)\) growing as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks. Ours has \(C(\varepsilon)\) about \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^3}\); random lists reach about \(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\).

Credit. The energy bound is Parseval's identity. The pigeonhole bound is Dirichlet's box argument for simultaneous approximation (1842). The strongest known lower bound, about \(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2 \log(1/\varepsilon)}\), comes from Alon's rank argument (2009); lower-bounds.md does not prove it. References

Part 11

The coding-theory view

The same two-layer idea is a classic way to build such lists for XOR instead of addition. An outer layer makes sure only a few branches can fail, and a small inner layer, found by search, balances every other branch.

The best XOR constructions add one more step. Trying that step here is the natural next project.
